# Section 135: AI Project Failure & Lessons Learned

> **110 interview questions and answers** covering AI Project Failure & Lessons Learned.

These answers are designed as concise interview-ready frameworks. Adapt them with your own examples, architecture choices, metrics, industry constraints, and company context rather than memorizing them word-for-word.

---

## Question 1

**Q: Tell me about a time problem formulation played an important role in an AI, software, data, product, or transformation initiative.**

**Answer:** I would answer this with a STAR structure and make problem formulation the decision or behavior at the center of the story. I would briefly set the context, explain the specific responsibility I owned, describe the actions I personally took, and quantify the result. I would include one trade-off or moment of judgment so the story demonstrates how I think, not just what the team delivered. For an AI-related role, I would connect the result to technical quality, customer impact, risk, or business value.

---

## Question 2

**Q: Describe a difficult situation where you had to manage problem formulation. What made it challenging and how did you respond?**

**Answer:** I would choose a real example where problem formulation was genuinely difficult because of incomplete information, competing priorities, technical constraints, or stakeholder pressure. I would explain how I reduced ambiguity, gathered evidence, made a decision, and kept others informed. The strongest story includes what I deliberately did not do and why. I would finish with the measurable outcome and what the experience changed about my approach.

---

## Question 3

**Q: Tell me about a decision involving problem formulation where important stakeholders disagreed. How did you handle it?**

**Answer:** For a conflict story about problem formulation, I would avoid framing the other person as the problem. I would describe the underlying disagreement—requirements, risk tolerance, architecture, timing, or business priority—then show how I surfaced assumptions and used evidence to create a shared decision framework. If compromise was appropriate, I would explain it; if I had to disagree and commit or escalate, I would say so. The result should show preserved trust as well as a decision.

---

## Question 4

**Q: Tell me about a time your approach to problem formulation did not work as expected. What did you learn?**

**Answer:** I would pick a failure involving problem formulation where I had meaningful ownership. I would state the miss clearly rather than disguising it as a success story, explain the root cause and my contribution to it, describe how I mitigated impact, and show the preventive change that followed. Good answers demonstrate accountability and learning. I would avoid blaming a model, vendor, requirement, or teammate for something I had the opportunity to manage.

---

## Question 5

**Q: Describe a situation where you had to balance problem formulation against competing priorities or constraints.**

**Answer:** For prioritization involving problem formulation, I would explain the competing demands and the criteria I used—customer impact, urgency, risk, strategic value, dependencies, effort, or reversibility. I would show how I made the trade-off visible to stakeholders and what I deferred. If new information changed the priority, I would explain how I adapted. The result should demonstrate decision quality, not simply that I worked longer hours.

## Question 6

**Q: Tell me about a time you influenced others on an issue involving problem formulation without relying on formal authority.**

**Answer:** To demonstrate influence around problem formulation, I would use an example where I lacked direct authority over at least one key stakeholder. I would show how I understood their incentives, built credibility, brought evidence, created a low-risk path to agreement, and adjusted my communication style. Influence is stronger when the other party retains ownership of the decision. I would quantify what changed because of the alignment.

## Question 7

**Q: How have you communicated an issue involving problem formulation to an audience with a very different level of technical knowledge?**

**Answer:** For communication involving problem formulation, I would explain how I changed the level of detail for the audience. With engineers I might discuss architecture, failure modes, and evidence; with executives I would focus on value, risk, trade-offs, and the decision required. I would describe how I checked for shared understanding rather than assuming a presentation was communication. A strong example includes a concrete decision or action that followed.

## Question 8

**Q: Give an example of how feedback or new evidence changed your approach to problem formulation.**

**Answer:** I would choose an example where feedback or new evidence materially changed my view of problem formulation. I would explain my original assumption, what data or feedback contradicted it, how I tested the new interpretation, and what I changed. This demonstrates intellectual honesty and adaptability. I would also mention how I updated the process, documentation, or monitoring so the learning persisted beyond one incident.

## Question 9

**Q: Describe how you would lead a team through an ambiguous or high-pressure situation where problem formulation is central.**

**Answer:** In a high-pressure situation involving problem formulation, I would first create clarity: immediate objective, decision owner, critical risks, available evidence, and communication cadence. I would separate stabilization from long-term improvement and delegate work through clear ownership. I would protect psychological safety so people surface bad news quickly. After the event, I would run a blameless review focused on systemic prevention and measurable follow-through.

## Question 10

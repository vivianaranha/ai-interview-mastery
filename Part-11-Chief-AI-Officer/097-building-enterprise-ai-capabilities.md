# Section 97: Building Enterprise AI Capabilities

> **110 interview questions and answers** covering Building Enterprise AI Capabilities.

These answers are designed as concise interview-ready frameworks. Adapt them with your own examples, architecture choices, metrics, industry constraints, and company context rather than memorizing them word-for-word.

---

## Question 1

**Q: Why does business requirements matter strategically in Building Enterprise AI Capabilities, and what enterprise outcome should leadership expect from it?**

**Answer:** Business requirements matters because measurable capabilities and constraints derived from business objectives and user needs. At an executive level, I would connect it to a strategic objective such as growth, productivity, customer experience, resilience, risk reduction, or faster decision-making. I would define the enterprise outcome first, then determine which AI, data, platform, talent, and governance capabilities are required. Leadership should expect a measurable change in business performance, not a count of models, pilots, or licenses.

---

## Question 2

**Q: How would you connect investment or executive attention around business requirements to measurable enterprise value?**

**Answer:** I would build the value case for business requirements from a baseline: current volume, cycle time, cost, quality, revenue, risk, or customer metric. Then I would estimate benefits, total cost of ownership, adoption requirements, time-to-value, and uncertainty. I would track realized value after launch rather than stop at an approved business case. AI activity is an input; enterprise value is the outcome.

---

## Question 3

**Q: What operating model, ownership, and decision rights are needed to manage business requirements effectively?**

**Answer:** For business requirements, I would define a federated operating model where enterprise standards and shared platforms coexist with domain ownership close to the business. Decision rights should cover strategy, funding, architecture, data, model risk, security, deployment, and value accountability. Every initiative needs a business owner and an accountable technical owner. Central teams should enable reuse and governance without becoming a bottleneck for every local decision.

---

## Question 4

**Q: What metrics would you put on an executive scorecard to track progress and outcomes related to business requirements?**

**Answer:** My executive scorecard for business requirements would mix value, adoption, delivery, risk, and operational measures. I would include realized benefit versus plan, active usage in target workflows, time-to-value, quality or task success, reliability, unit cost, material incidents, high-risk findings, and remediation status. I would segment metrics by portfolio or business domain so averages do not hide weak areas. Each metric should have an owner and a decision attached to it.

---

## Question 5

**Q: What are the most material risks associated with business requirements, and how would you govern them?**

**Answer:** The most material risks around business requirements can include inaccurate decisions, data leakage, cyber abuse, regulatory exposure, third-party dependency, bias, uncontrolled autonomy, operational failure, cost escalation, and reputational harm. I would tier governance by impact and require stronger testing, approval, monitoring, and human oversight for higher-risk uses. Risk acceptance must sit with an accountable business executive, supported by independent challenge where appropriate.

---

## Question 6

**Q: How would you align business, technology, data, security, legal, finance, and risk stakeholders on decisions involving business requirements?**

**Answer:** Alignment around business requirements starts with one shared problem statement and decision framework. Business leaders should own outcomes; technology and data teams should own delivery capabilities; security, privacy, legal, finance, and risk should shape constraints and independent controls. I would surface conflicts early—speed versus control, build versus buy, central versus federated, innovation versus standardization—and make the trade-off explicit. Executive governance should resolve decisions, not merely exchange status updates.

---

## Question 7

**Q: How would business requirements affect portfolio prioritization and funding decisions when resources are constrained?**

**Answer:** When resources are constrained, I would rank work on business requirements by strategic alignment, expected value, feasibility, risk, dependency, reuse potential, and time-to-value. I would protect a small amount of capacity for experimentation but require evidence before scaling investment. Shared platforms should be funded when they unlock multiple high-value use cases, not because platform work is fashionable. I would stop or pause initiatives that repeatedly fail to prove value.

---

## Question 8

**Q: What adoption, talent, process, or change-management considerations are important when business requirements is a major enterprise priority?**

**Answer:** Business requirements succeeds only if the organization changes how work gets done. I would identify affected roles, process redesign, skill gaps, incentives, communications, training, support, and managerial accountability. Adoption metrics should be built into the business case. For high-impact change, I would involve users early and redesign workflows around human + AI strengths rather than simply adding an AI interface to an unchanged process.

---

## Question 9

**Q: An executive sponsor wants to accelerate work involving business requirements despite unresolved risks and weak evidence of value. What would you do?**

**Answer:** I would separate urgency from evidence. I would show the sponsor what is unresolved around business requirements, the downside if the risk materializes, and the current evidence of value. Then I would offer a bounded path forward—pilot, shadow mode, human approval, limited data, a small cohort, or staged investment—with explicit exit criteria. If the residual risk exceeds enterprise tolerance, I would escalate the decision rather than let enthusiasm silently become risk acceptance.

---

## Question 10

**Answer:** To the CEO or board, I would frame business requirements around four questions: What strategic outcome are we pursuing? What value have we realized? What material risks or dependencies could change the outcome? What decision or investment is required now? I would use a small number of trend metrics and compare them with targets. Technical detail belongs in the appendix unless it materially changes value, risk, or the decision.
